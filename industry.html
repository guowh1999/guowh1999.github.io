Case studies

01

TIL therapy

Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes — one approval, a filed NDA in China, and eight programmes in the clinic. 1 approved (US) China NDA filed 8 in clinic

TIL therapy takes the lymphocytes already living inside a tumour, expands them outside the body, and gives them back after lymphodepletion. It is the first cell therapy that works in solid tumours — and the first to show how much of the difficulty is logistics rather than biology.

Where it stands
  1. Feb 2024 — first approval The FDA granted accelerated approval to lifileucel (Amtagvi, Iovance) in advanced melanoma. It remains the only approved TIL product: list price $515,000, a boxed warning, and 7.5% treatment-related mortality in the study population.
  2. 2025 — the commercial check Iovance cut its full-year revenue guidance from $475m to $300m and the stock fell 45% in a day. The efficacy is real; so is the burden of lymphodepletion and high-dose IL-2.
  3. Oct 2026 — China files first NMPA accepted Juncell's GC101 (nogileucel), combined with sintilimab, for melanoma after at least two systemic lines including a PD-1 antibody. It is the first TIL NDA submitted in China.

What each programme is betting on
Iovance · lifileucel (Amtagvi)
The reference product: unmodified TIL, non-myeloablative lymphodepletion, high-dose IL-2. The bet was manufacturing at scale; the constraint turned out to be the preconditioning regimen, not the cell.
Juncell · GC101 (nogileucel)
Built by removing the two hardest steps — no lymphodepletion, no IL-2 (DeepTIL). In the pivotal MIZAR-003 study median PFS was 4.37 vs 1.61 months (HR 0.44) and ORR 44% vs 8.2%, in a population dominated by mucosal and acral melanoma. China's first TIL NDA, accepted October 2026.
Grit · GT101
The first Chinese TIL to enter the clinic. Pivotal Phase II in cervical cancer, where Phase I reported a 45.5% ORR.
Grit · GT201
Membrane-bound IL-15 engineered TIL: persistence without IL-2. The case that travels is a floor-of-mouth squamous carcinoma — partial response at three weeks, complete response at nine.
BlueHorse · LM103
ASCO 2025, twelve evaluable melanoma patients: ORR 41.7%, DCR 83.3%. Now in a randomised Phase II against investigator's-choice chemotherapy.
Huasai · HS-IT101 (FAST-TIL)
Manufacturing as the product: faster expansion, automation, higher stem-cell fraction, cost claimed at roughly a tenth of Amtagvi. Phase I in NSCLC (FASTIL-Lung01).
Bennu · engineered TIL
Gene-edited TIL aimed squarely at exhaustion, from their FOST platform; NSCLC recruiting. Early, but the editing angle is the one to watch.
BioSyngen · BST02
Takes TIL into liver cancer, where most patients cannot tolerate full lymphodepletion. Phase I in locally advanced HCC.

China policy & market access

02

Commercialising an innovative drug in China

Seven gates between approval and a patient — each with a different regulator, a different clock and its own paperwork. 7 stages NRDL: one cycle a year Two catalogues since 2026

An approval is a beginning, not an endpoint. In China the distance between a drug registration certificate and a reimbursed prescription runs through pricing, provincial listing, a national negotiation, and then one hospital at a time. Each gate has its own owner and its own calendar, and they do not wait for each other.

Stage by stage
  1. Clinical development & IND

    1–3 years · CDE, ethics committees, trial sites

    • File the IND with CDE. If nothing comes back within 60 working days, it proceeds.
    • Design for the registration pathway from day one — breakthrough designation, priority review, or conditional approval on a single-arm Phase II.
    • Book the pre-NDA meeting. The package that gets accepted is the one agreed in advance.
    • In oncology, the comparator and endpoint chosen here are the ones defended at the price negotiation later.
  2. NDA review

    200 working days · 130 under priority review

    • Submit, then expect a deficiency letter (发补) that stops the clock. The calendar is usually set by how fast you answer.
    • Registration inspection (GCP/GMP, on site) and sample testing run in parallel. Neither is a formality.
    • Approval issues the drug registration certificate. The marketing authorisation holder — not the plant — carries the liability.
  3. Launch & price listing

    1–3 months · 31+ provincial platforms

    • Set the launch price. Most innovative drugs are company-priced, but this number anchors every later negotiation.
    • List on each provincial procurement platform: separate windows, separate documents.
    • Obtain the national drug code. Without it, nothing downstream can be reimbursed.
    • Choose distributors under the two-invoice rule; imported products also clear customs.
  4. NRDL negotiation

    ~12 months · one cycle a year

    • The national catalogue is revised once a year. Miss the window and the drug waits another twelve months.
    • Since 2026, pre-application is allowed: a drug whose technical review is complete but whose approval is not yet issued can enter the same cycle — worth 3–6 months.
    • Build the dossier on budget impact, comparator choice and health economics. This is a price-per-patient argument, not a clinical one.
    • Two outcomes: an agreed price with a two-year agreement, or no listing and a self-pay route.
  5. Hospital listing

    3–12 months per hospital

    • Every hospital's pharmacy committee decides separately, and some meet only once a year.
    • Negotiated drugs are exempt from the one-molecule-two-brands rule and from drug-share assessment — helpful, but not a mandate to list.
    • Dual channel: designated retail pharmacies can dispense and be reimbursed, which is often how patients get the drug before the hospital does.
    • Under DRG/DIP, new technologies can apply to be excluded from the payment bundle.
  6. Prescription & patient access

    continuous

    • Guideline inclusion (CSCO and specialty societies) changes prescribing; the approval letter does not.
    • Patient assistance programmes, 惠民保 and commercial insurance cover the gap before and after reimbursement.
    • DTP pharmacies and prescription outflow do much of the actual dispensing.
  7. Lifecycle

    years

    • New indications go in as supplementary applications, then through the catalogue again.
    • After eight years in the agreement, a drug moves to the routine catalogue with a defined cut: simple renewal, or 10 percentage points if annual spend exceeds ¥300m.
    • Watch the biosimilar and volume-based procurement clock. It starts earlier than most teams plan for.

Provincial rollout

The 2026 cycle started a month earlier than usual, with the final list due before the end of November and effective on 1 January. The compressed calendar is deliberate: provinces need time to update systems, list products and design insurance products.

Three payment routes
Direct Basic medical insurance

Reasonably priced innovative drugs negotiate straight into the national catalogue.

Bridge Commercial innovative drug catalogue

High-priced drugs outside basic insurance enter the commercial catalogue first — new in 2026, and already a reference point for insurers and mutual-aid schemes.

Return Back to basic insurance

Once evidence and price converge, a drug in the commercial catalogue can apply directly to the national list.

Where it usually stalls
  • Approval is not access. A hospital listing can take longer than the NDA review did.
  • The launch price is not the price. Each negotiation resets the reference, and renewal resets it again.
  • Two catalogues, two clocks. Basic insurance and the commercial catalogue are reviewed on different tracks.
  • The last kilometre is provincial. Thirty-one platforms, thirty-one document sets, thirty-one timelines.

Sources: NHSA 2026 catalogue adjustment plan (draft, May 2026); 21st Century Business Herald reporting on the 2026 cycle; NMPA/CDE review timelines; provincial procurement platform rules. Snapshot as of May 2026 — timelines and thresholds change with each annual cycle.

03

Inside an NRDL negotiation

Formal review, expert scoring, a secret envelope price, then two rounds of bargaining — the machinery behind one line in the catalogue. 5 gates CEA + BIA Envelope price

The catalogue is not a price list a company applies to join. It is the output of a five-stage exercise in which a panel decides, in advance and in secret, the highest price it is willing to pay — and then negotiates against that number. Almost all of the economics happens before anyone sits down.

The five gates
01 · JulApplications 02 · Jul–AugFormal review 03 · Aug–SepExpert review 04 · Sep–OctPricing calculation 05 · Oct–NovNegotiation 06 · Nov–DecAgreement
Stage by stage
  1. Applications & formal review

    July · pass or fail, no scoring

    • Everything goes through the national insurance platform — no paper, no courier.
    • Formal review is binary. It checks eligibility and completeness; it does not judge value.
    • The summary slides get a separate content check. Fail it and the experts review the file without your slides.
    • Drugs that pass are published for comment — but without the economic information. Comments are checked, then the final list is announced.
  2. Expert review

    August · one month, multi-disciplinary

    • Clinical, pharmacy, pharmacoeconomics and insurance-management experts sit in one room and score together, rather than in sequence.
    • Dimensions: efficacy, safety, fairness, innovativeness. Both collective discussion and individual scoring are recorded.
    • Output is four lists: direct inclusion, inclusion via negotiation, removal, and renewal.
    • For every negotiated drug the panel also fixes the reference product, the reimbursed scope and the standard pack — decisions that move the arithmetic more than the trial data does.
  3. Pricing calculation — the envelope

    September–October · closed door

    • Two separate teams: a fund-impact group (基金测算组) and a pharmacoeconomics group (药物经济学测算组).
    • They compute a ceiling price, seal it in an envelope, and it stays inside the room.
    • The company may talk to the team lead one-to-one to explain assumptions — but never sees the number.
    • This is where cost-effectiveness and budget impact do their work; the next two sections are the arithmetic.
  4. Negotiation

    October–November · usually one day

    • Two rounds of offer. The company names a price; the panel answers yes or no against the envelope.
    • A final offer at or below the ceiling signs the deal. Above it, the drug leaves without coverage.
    • The result is confirmed in writing on the spot; the agreed price becomes the national payment standard.
    • This is a game against an unknown number, not a negotiation over the drug's merits — the merits were settled in August.
  5. Agreement & publication

    November–December

    • A two-year agreement, one national payment standard, effective 1 January.
    • Renewal later runs on the ratio rules below — not on a fresh negotiation.
Cost-effectiveness: is it worth it at any price?

The pharmacoeconomics group asks a single question in cost per unit of health gained — normally cost per quality-adjusted life-year. The instrument is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio.

ICER= C₁ − C₀E₁ − E₀ = ΔCΔE

ΔC is the total cost difference — drug, administration, monitoring, minus the care the new drug avoids. ΔE is the health gain, usually in QALYs. Costs are discounted at 5% a year and the model runs over a lifetime horizon.

Pmax= k · ΔE − ΔCother

Inverted, the ratio gives the ceiling a company can charge. k is the willingness-to-pay threshold — China's pharmacoeconomic guideline points at 1–3× GDP per capita per QALY, and the panel treats it as a reference, not a rule. ΔCother is every cost except the drug price itself. This is the number that decides how much of the value the price is allowed to capture.

Budget impact: can the fund afford it?

Cost-effectiveness says nothing about the size of the bill. The fund-impact group prices the whole population instead of the average patient.

ΔBt= Σs ( Ns,t · us,t ) · ( P + cs − Ccomp,s )

N is the eligible population in segment s, u the uptake rate, P the price under discussion, c the other costs of using the drug, Ccomp the cost of current standard care. The constraint is simply max ΔBt ≤ Bcap: no year may exceed the affordability line the fund sets for that year.

CEA asks

Is this price fair per unit of health gained, against this comparator?

BIA asks

Is the total bill payable, in this year, for this many patients?

A drug can pass CEA and fail BIA. When that happens the envelope is not a rejection — it is simply set lower, at the price the fund can carry.

The envelope price
P*= min ( PCEA, PBIA, Pintl )

Three ceilings and the lowest one wins: the value-based price from CEA, the affordability-based price from BIA, and an international reference price. The ministry does not publish the calculation, so this is the structure the literature and the submitted dossiers describe rather than a published formula — but it explains what the panel is doing behind the envelope.

After the deal: the renewal ratio

A negotiated price is a two-year price. At renewal the cut is mechanical, driven by how far actual fund spending ran above the budget the company itself forecast.

A= actual fund spendingbudgeted fund spending , B= Δ budget from scope expansionmax( budget , actual )

Actual spending is not the audited figure for the year: it is last year's full-year spend + this year's first-half spend × 2 — an annualisation the rules state explicitly.
